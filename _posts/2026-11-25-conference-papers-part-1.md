Conference papers are the orphans of academia. We bring them into the world in a hurry, usually a few hours before the deadline, we dress them in a two-column template, and after the conference nobody asks about them again. Not the readers, not the reviewers, and often not even the authors.

This is the first of two posts about conferences. Part 1 is about the papers and the sessions. I will write part 2 when I go to a conference in Korea.

## Nobody cares

A journal paper has a life. Reviewers argue with you, you answer, you repeat the measurement, and months later you have something you can defend. People cite it, because it is the version that counts.

A conference paper has a deadline. Often the abstract is accepted before the work is finished, and the rest of the story is written around whatever results exist on submission day. After that it goes into a database, between a thousand other papers, and waits.

There is also an open secret: most of us do not put the real contribution in it. If an idea is strong, we save it for the journal. The conference gets the first version, the side result, the comparison we had to make anyway, or the topic that did not fit anywhere else. Everyone knows this, so everyone reads conference papers with that in mind, if they read them at all.

> This is not true for every field. In computer science a top conference can matter more than a journal. Here I am talking about power electronics.

## So why do we write them?

Because a conference paper is also a ticket.

With an accepted paper, your university pays for you to go somewhere you would never have gone otherwise. You see a new city. You meet the person whose papers you have been citing for three years, and you find out that they are funny, or shy, or wrong about something. You have a conversation in a coffee break that saves you two months. None of this is in the paper, but the paper is the reason you are there.

There is a less romantic reason too. A deadline forces you to finish. Many results would stay in a folder forever without one.

## Oral or poster?

When the acceptance e-mail arrives, the first thing people check is whether it is an oral or a poster. Oral sounds more prestigious. I am not sure it is better.

| | Oral | Poster |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Time | A short talk and a few questions | An hour or two of standing |
| Audience | Many people, half of them looking at their own slides | Few people, but they chose to come |
| Questions | One or two, sometimes none | As many as you can survive |
| What you get | Practice and a line in the CV | Real discussions |
| Worst case | The projector does not work | Nobody stops |

In an oral session you speak to a room, the chair thanks you, and it is over. In a poster session you explain the same figure twenty times, and by the twentieth time you finally understand it yourself. If you want feedback, the poster is the better deal. If you want people to see your face, take the oral.

## The plenary session

Then there is the plenary: the biggest room, the best speakers, and the only hour when the whole conference sits in one place. Nobody talks about equation 14. The speaker talks about where the field is going, what industry wants, and what we will all be writing in our introductions next year.

It is easy to skip, especially the one on the first morning after a long flight. I think it is the session to keep. The paper you can read later at home. The plenary shows you what people think is important right now.

## Big or small?

A big conference has everything: parallel sessions, an exhibition, free pens, famous names. It also has the problem that two talks you want to see are always at the same time, in rooms at opposite ends of the building. You can spend three days there and speak only with the people you came with.

A small conference has one or two rooms. You see every talk, you eat at the same tables, and by the second day you know everyone. It looks less impressive in a CV. But people remember you, and that is worth more.

If you are at the start of your PhD, I would say: go to a big one once to see how large the field is, and go to a small one to become part of it.
