METU is one of the best technical universities in Turkey. Its graduates work all over the world, and its students are as good as anywhere I have seen. But on the research side, METU does not compete with the leading universities of the world. For a long time I could not understand why.

So what makes one group's research better than another's? I have now worked in two labs: [PowerLab](https://odtu.github.io/) at METU ([photos](https://odtu.github.io/pictures)), and the [Sustainable Power Laboratory](https://spl.eecs.kth.se/facilities/) at KTH, where most of my work happens in the [power electronics laboratory](https://spl.eecs.kth.se/power-electronics-laboratory/). I want to compare them from the inside.

## Why the lab matters so much in power electronics

In some fields you can do excellent research with a laptop. Power electronics is not one of them.

Our best journals expect experimental results. A new converter, modulation method or control scheme is not convincing until it runs on a prototype, with measured waveforms and efficiency. Simulation alone rarely gets you into a top journal.

Power electronics is also an industrial field. When a company funds a project, it wants to see the idea work on real hardware before it believes it. So the lab is not only where we test our ideas. It is how we earn trust, from reviewers and from industry.

If the lab is the key, I expected the lab to explain the gap.

## The labs are not that different

When I came to KTH, I expected a different world. It was not.

Yes, there are more and better tools. The KTH lab has [ten facilities](https://spl.eecs.kth.se/facilities/), from a 200 kW flexible power supply platform to double-pulse testing of semiconductors up to 10 kV. There are instruments I did not have before. But there is no huge difference, and honestly, I do not even use many of them. Most of the work happens with the same things in both places: an oscilloscope, probes, power supplies, a load, a PCB and a lot of patience.

So the tools are not the answer either.

## The real difference: everything around the lab

What surprised me at KTH was not the equipment. It was how easy it is to build things.

Prototyping is simple. You need components, you order them. You need a PCB, you order it. Every expense is covered by a project, and the process does not stand between you and your work. A design can turn into hardware in days.

At METU, the same steps were a fight.

The first problem is customs. Components and PCBs often come from abroad, and getting them through customs takes time, paperwork and luck. A board you need this week may arrive in a month.

The second problem is funding. It was often unclear which budget would pay for what, and which project an expense belonged to. Nobody really knew. Before you can buy a component, you have to find out who can pay for it.

None of this is visible in a paper. But every week lost in customs or in finding the right budget is a week without experiments, and in our field, no experiments means no paper.

## And yet

Still, we managed. With patience and some creativity, the output from METU can be similar to what comes out of well-funded labs in Europe. I have seen papers from Ankara that are as good as any.

But look at the citations, and you will see that we are not competing.

## The part I do not understand

I think part of the answer has nothing to do with labs. Academia likes to see itself as objective, but it is full of bias. There are big names and big universities, and people want to collaborate with them, cite them and trust their results, because they assume those groups are better. A paper from a famous lab gets read. The same paper from a less known university has to work much harder to be noticed.

Is this fair? I do not think so. Is it the whole story? I do not know. Maybe reputation follows quality, and quality follows money, time and easy prototyping. Maybe reputation also creates itself.

What I do know is that the gap between METU and KTH is not in the oscilloscopes. It is in how much of a researcher's time goes into research and unfortunate bias from peers.
